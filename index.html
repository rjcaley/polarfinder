Steps 1 and 2 are done together, in one five-minute session at a puddle or a glass tabletop. Neither needs the sun, a compass, or a GPS fix. Declination is now computed automatically from your location and follows you as you travel.

Step 1 · Where is the filter's axis?

Your filter's engraving has no fixed relationship to its transmission axis — B+W does not index them. This one measurement ties the two together, and only has to be done once. No phone sensors needed.
  1. Find a horizontal, shiny, non-metal surface with a reflection on it: a puddle, a glass tabletop, a polished wood table, a car's paint, a wet road.
  2. Stand back so you are looking down at the reflection at a shallow-ish angle — roughly 30–40° below horizontal is ideal. Keep your head level.
  3. Hold the filter up to your eye the right way round: the front (engraved) face toward the reflection, the back toward your eye. A circular polarizer only works in that direction.
  4. Rotate the filter slowly. The reflection will get strongly darker at one point and brightest 90° away. Park it at the darkest point.
  5. Without rotating it further, read which marking is at the top of the filter and enter it below. Use the dial to match what you see.
Bracket the minimum — do not eyeball it. A polarizer minimum is flat: five degrees either side of perfect changes the brightness by about 0.2%, which no eye can see. Twenty degrees off, the brightness is changing twenty times faster. So instead of hunting for "darkest":

1. Turn well past the dark point until the reflection is clearly coming back — note that position as A.
2. Turn back the other way until it looks equally bright — that is B.
3. The true minimum is exactly halfway between. Enter both and the app halves them.

Simulated, this is about 4× more accurate than judging the darkest point directly (median 1.2° against 4.7°).
Or drag the dial below to the darkest point directly, if you prefer.

Step 2 · Handedness — usually skippable

The sign is forced by the geometry. Given the ring drawing matches your filter, +1 is the only possibility: the end-to-end simulation puts it on the true intensity minimum at 100.0000%, while the mirrored version manages 54.6%.

The five-second check that actually matters: compare the ring drawing on the Aim tab with your filter. Does the engraving run the same way round on both — “46” near the top, then KSM, then MASTER, then B+W MADE IN GERMANY going clockwise? If yes, handedness is right; leave this alone.

The screen test below is belt-and-braces if you want it.
Nearly every LCD and OLED emits linearly polarised light through a bonded polariser, so the polarisation turns with the device. A few screens add a retarder film on top so they do not black out through polarised sunglasses; on those there is no clear minimum and an LCD laptop screen is the reliable fallback. Turn the screen and the correct filter position must turn with it: the same way round if the handedness is as assumed, the opposite way if it is mirrored.

You take two readings. The app compares them. It does not matter what the screen's own polarisation angle is, and the turn does not have to be exactly 45° — but which way you turn it is critical, so set that below.
  1. Plain white full-screen on a second device — tablet, spare phone, laptop. Dim the room.
  2. Hold it upright facing you, head level. Rotate the filter to the darkest point, set the dial to the marking at the top, and tap Capture 1.
  3. Now turn the screen so its top edge moves toward your right, by roughly 45° — about halfway to portrait. Keep the face pointed at you.
  4. Re-find the darkest point, set the dial again, tap Capture 2.

Declination · automatic, nothing to do

The app carries the World Magnetic Model 2025, the NOAA/BGS model that ships in aircraft and marine navigation systems. It computes the magnetic-to-true north correction for wherever you are standing, to about 0.4°, and recomputes it as you move. Travel changes nothing and there is no calibration to redo. The model is valid through December 2029.

Compass truth check (GPS) — only needed in compass mode

Chrome on Android reports headings from Android's rotation-vector sensor, which is referenced to magnetic north — so the app applies the declination above. Some Android layers hand back true north already, in which case it should not. This is a property of your phone and browser, not your location, so checking it once settles it permanently.

The check uses GPS course over ground, which comes from satellites and knows nothing about magnetism. No sun, no flat ground, no straight objects — just walk.
  1. Get outside with a clear sky view and a straight run of at least 50 m — a path, a quiet pavement, an empty car park.
  2. Hold the phone in front of you, screen toward you, back facing the way you will walk.
  3. Tap Start, walk straight at a normal pace for about 30 seconds, then tap Stop.

Site correction

A magnetometer cannot diagnose its own site. Field magnitude and dip give two numbers; the anomaly has three components, and the one that rotates your heading is invisible to both. That is an information limit, not a missing feature — spinning the phone does not help either, because at a fixed spot the disturbed field is one fixed vector.

An independent heading reference breaks the deadlock, and you are carrying one: the filter. Building steel disturbs the field at a spot, so the heading error there is a constant — take one optical fix and every other direction from that spot is corrected too.

The one rule

Point the filter's transmission axis at the sun's position projected into your frame. That is the whole thing. Everything below is why.

Why that works

Light that reaches your lens from blue sky got there by scattering off air molecules. Light that makes leaves and paint look glossy got there by glancing off a surface. Both are single-bounce events, and both obey the same rule: the scattered light's electric field ends up perpendicular to the scattering plane — the plane containing the sun, the thing doing the scattering, and you.

A polarizer passes light whose field is along its transmission axis and blocks light perpendicular to it. So to kill the scattered light you want the axis perpendicular to that field, which means the axis must lie in the scattering plane. Project the sun's direction onto your image plane and you have it.

The nice consequence: sky darkening and sheen removal want the same filter angle, because they share the same sun and the same viewing direction. You are never trading one against the other.

How much you get

For clear sky the Rayleigh model gives degree of polarization P = Pmax·sin²γ / (1 + cos²γ), where γ is the angle between the sun and where you are pointing. It peaks at γ = 90° — the famous band across the sky at right angles to the sun — and falls to zero looking straight at or straight away from the sun. Real skies reach roughly Pmax ≈ 0.75 rather than 1.0, because of multiple scattering, aerosols and ground reflection; humid or hazy air pushes it lower still.

Specular sheen off leaves and paint follows Fresnel instead. Its polarization peaks where light strikes the surface near Brewster's angle, about 55° for waxy leaf cuticle and clear coat, which corresponds to γ ≈ 68°. Because leaves point every which way, the effect stays useful across a broad range of γ — call it 40° to 120°.

Water and glass reflections are a special case you barely need this app for: those surfaces are usually horizontal, so the reflected light is horizontally polarized and the axis wants to be vertical in the frame. That is also exactly the geometry used to calibrate the filter in Step 1.

What the phone contributes

GPS + clock give the sun's azimuth and altitude from a standard solar position algorithm. Location only needs to be right to a few kilometres; time needs to be right to about a minute.

Compass + tilt give where the lens is pointed. This is the weak link: Android's rotation vector is referenced to magnetic north and it drifts near metal. Declination is handled automatically by the embedded World Magnetic Model. What no model can fix is a compass that is wrong by a constant amount — a stale magnetometer calibration or building steel — which is what the GPS walk check is for.

Roll is the other input, and the app assumes a level horizon unless you tell it otherwise, since that is how an M gets held most of the time.

Honest limits

Polarization angle varies across a wide frame. On a 35mm lens the correct angle can differ by several degrees between the left and right edge, and on a 21mm by a lot more — plus wide skies show the polarization band as an obvious dark patch. The app solves for the centre of the frame.

An error of ±10° in the ring costs you very little: transmitted intensity goes as cos² of the error, so 10° off still removes 97% of what a perfect setting would. ±20° still gets 88%. So do not agonise — this is a forgiving instrument.

The circular polarizer must face the right way. Engraved side toward the subject. Backwards, the quarter-wave plate scrambles the light before it reaches the linear element and nothing works.
