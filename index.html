Step 1 · Where is the filter's axis?

Your filter's engraving has no fixed relationship to its transmission axis — B+W does not index them. This one measurement ties the two together, and only has to be done once. No phone sensors needed.
  1. Find a horizontal, shiny, non-metal surface with a reflection on it: a puddle, a glass tabletop, a polished wood table, a car's paint, a wet road.
  2. Stand back so you are looking down at the reflection at a shallow-ish angle — roughly 30–40° below horizontal is ideal. Keep your head level.
  3. Hold the filter up to your eye the right way round: the front (engraved) face toward the reflection, the back toward your eye. A circular polarizer only works in that direction.
  4. Rotate the filter slowly. The reflection will get strongly darker at one point and brightest 90° away. Park it at the darkest point.
  5. Without rotating it further, read which marking is at the top of the filter and enter it below. Use the dial to match what you see.

Step 2 · Compass offset (shadow sight)

Phone magnetometers read magnetic north, drift near the brass and steel of an M-body, and Chrome does not tell us your magnetic declination. One sun sight fixes all three at once. Redo this if you travel a long way or the readings look wrong. Never sight the sun by eye.
  1. Pick a sunny moment and a flat patch of ground. Stand a pen, bottle or post upright on it.
  2. The shadow points directly away from the sun. Stand at the tip of the shadow.
  3. Hold the phone upright, screen toward you, and aim its back squarely along the shadow toward the object — that is, toward the sun, but keep your eyes on the screen.
  4. Hold steady and tap Capture. Do it three or four times and the app will average them.

Step 3 · Handedness check

There are two ways the ring's rotation could map onto the sky, and they differ by a mirror. Step 1 cannot tell them apart. This test can. You need the sun roughly 45° off to one side of what you are photographing and well above the horizon — mid-morning or mid-afternoon, aimed at blue sky. The one rule

Point the filter's transmission axis at the sun's position projected into your frame. That is the whole thing. Everything below is why.

Why that works

Light that reaches your lens from blue sky got there by scattering off air molecules. Light that makes leaves and paint look glossy got there by glancing off a surface. Both are single-bounce events, and both obey the same rule: the scattered light's electric field ends up perpendicular to the scattering plane — the plane containing the sun, the thing doing the scattering, and you.

A polarizer passes light whose field is along its transmission axis and blocks light perpendicular to it. So to kill the scattered light you want the axis perpendicular to that field, which means the axis must lie in the scattering plane. Project the sun's direction onto your image plane and you have it.

The nice consequence: sky darkening and sheen removal want the same filter angle, because they share the same sun and the same viewing direction. You are never trading one against the other.

How much you get

For clear sky the Rayleigh model gives degree of polarization P = Pmax·sin²γ / (1 + cos²γ), where γ is the angle between the sun and where you are pointing. It peaks at γ = 90° — the famous band across the sky at right angles to the sun — and falls to zero looking straight at or straight away from the sun. Real skies reach roughly Pmax ≈ 0.75 rather than 1.0, because of multiple scattering, aerosols and ground reflection; humid or hazy air pushes it lower still.

Specular sheen off leaves and paint follows Fresnel instead. Its polarization peaks where light strikes the surface near Brewster's angle, about 55° for waxy leaf cuticle and clear coat, which corresponds to γ ≈ 68°. Because leaves point every which way, the effect stays useful across a broad range of γ — call it 40° to 120°.

Water and glass reflections are a special case you barely need this app for: those surfaces are usually horizontal, so the reflected light is horizontally polarized and the axis wants to be vertical in the frame. That is also exactly the geometry used to calibrate the filter in Step 1.

What the phone contributes

GPS + clock give the sun's azimuth and altitude from a standard solar position algorithm. Location only needs to be right to a few kilometres; time needs to be right to about a minute.

Compass + tilt give where the lens is pointed. This is the weak link: Android's rotation vector is referenced to magnetic north, it drifts near metal, and the app has no way to read your declination — which is why the shadow sight in Step 2 exists.

Roll is the other input, and the app assumes a level horizon unless you tell it otherwise, since that is how an M gets held most of the time.

Honest limits

Polarization angle varies across a wide frame. On a 35mm lens the correct angle can differ by several degrees between the left and right edge, and on a 21mm by a lot more — plus wide skies show the polarization band as an obvious dark patch. The app solves for the centre of the frame.

An error of ±10° in the ring costs you very little: transmitted intensity goes as cos² of the error, so 10° off still removes 97% of what a perfect setting would. ±20° still gets 88%. So do not agonise — this is a forgiving instrument.

The circular polarizer must face the right way. Engraved side toward the subject. Backwards, the quarter-wave plate scrambles the light before it reaches the linear element and nothing works.
